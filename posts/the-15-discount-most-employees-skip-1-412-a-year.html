The 15% Discount Most Employees Skip: $1,412 a Year

Money Intel Daily · July 31, 2026

An employee stock purchase plan that buys at a 15% discount is a 17.6% return on the money you put in — before the market does anything.

▶ Watch this breakdown on YouTube

An employee stock purchase plan that buys at a 15% discount is a 17.6% return on the money you put in — before the market does anything. On an $80,000 salary contributing 10%, that is $1,412 a year captured from the discount alone, and roughly 38% annualized across two six-month periods.

The Number That Changes Everything

A fifteen percent discount is not a fifteen percent return. You pay eighty-five dollars for something worth a hundred, so your gain on the money you actually spent is seventeen point six percent. Do that twice a year and the annualized figure is thirty-eight point four percent. On an eighty thousand dollar salary putting ten percent through the plan, that's fourteen hundred twelve dollars a year taken out of the discount alone, before the share price moves a cent.

Why This Matters

Meet Priya. Software support, eighty thousand a year, and an employee stock purchase plan she ignored for four years because the enrolment email had the word "stock" in it and she had decided she wasn't a stock person. She wasn't wrong to be cautious. She was wrong about what she was declining.

What the plan actually offered was this: payroll deducts money for six months, then buys company shares at fifteen percent below market, and she can sell them the same day. Four years of that is roughly five thousand six hundred dollars she left with her employer, in exchange for avoiding a risk she could have closed out within twenty-four hours.

How It Actually Works

The trap in an ESPP isn't the discount. It's the two things people do after the discount.

The first is holding. The shares land in your account, the company is doing fine, someone in the break room says it's about to run, and the sensible plan of selling on day one becomes a position you're emotionally attached to. Now your salary and your savings depend on the same company. That's the concentration risk that turns a guaranteed seventeen percent into whatever happens next.

The second is the tax surprise. In a qualified plan the discount itself gets taxed as ordinary income in the year you sell — it's compensation, not a capital gain. That doesn't make the trade bad. It makes it a raise, and raises get taxed. People who don't know that feel cheated in April and stop participating.

The Real Numbers (Run For You)

Eighty thousand salary, ten percent contribution: eight thousand dollars a year, four thousand per six-month period.

Buy at eighty-five percent of market, sell at market: your gain is fifteen divided by eighty-five, which is seventeen point six five percent of what you spent. Four thousand times that is seven hundred six dollars per period. Two periods a year: fourteen hundred twelve dollars.

Now take only that discount — not the eight thousand, just the fourteen hundred twelve you didn't have before — and invest it every year for thirty years at seven percent. One hundred thirty-three thousand, three hundred fifty-six dollars.

And that figure is conservative, because it assumes your money sits for the full six months. In reality payroll deducts it gradually, so the average dollar is only in the plan for about three months, which makes the true annualized return higher than thirty-eight percent.

What To Do Today

Open your benefits portal and search for the letters E-S-P-P. If it exists, find two numbers: the discount percentage and the enrolment deadline. If there's a discount and you can sell immediately, contribute up to the point where the payroll deduction still lets you pay your bills — and write the sale date in your calendar the same day you enrol, so the decision to sell is already made before the shares ever show up.
