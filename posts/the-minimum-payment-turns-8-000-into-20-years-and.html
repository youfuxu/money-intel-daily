The Minimum Payment Turns $8,000 Into 20 Years and $14,760

Money Intel Daily · July 28, 2026

The minimum payment is not a payment plan — it is a product, and it is designed to keep you paying.

▶ Watch this breakdown on YouTube

The minimum payment is not a payment plan — it is a product, and it is designed to keep you paying. An $8,000 balance at 24.5% APR, paid at the standard 1%-plus-interest minimum, takes 20.7 years and costs $14,760 in interest.

The Number That Changes Everything

Eight thousand dollars on a credit card at twenty-four point five percent. Pay the minimum every month, exactly as the statement asks, and you are out of debt in twenty point seven years, having paid fourteen thousand, seven hundred sixty dollars in interest. Pay a flat three hundred dollars a month instead — about ninety dollars more than that first minimum — and you are done in three point two years, having paid three thousand, six hundred seventy-one dollars. Same debt. Same card. Eleven thousand dollars and seventeen years apart.

Why This Matters

Meet Jordan. He's twenty-eight, the eight thousand came from a move, a laptop, and one bad month, and every statement tells him the minimum is two hundred forty-seven dollars. He pays it. Sometimes he pays a little extra. The balance keeps looking roughly the same, and he has quietly concluded he's just bad with money. He isn't. He's paying exactly what he was asked to pay, and what he was asked to pay was designed to make this take two decades.

How It Actually Works

Here's the design. Most issuers set the minimum at one percent of the balance plus that month's interest. Read that again: the interest is included in the minimum. So the minimum is almost exactly the amount that stops the balance from growing, plus one percent of progress. Early on, at twenty-four point five percent, that eight thousand generates about a hundred sixty-three dollars of interest a month, so out of a two hundred forty-seven dollar payment, only around eighty dollars actually reduces what you owe. And because the minimum is a percentage, it shrinks as the balance shrinks — the payment gets smaller exactly when you'd want it to stay big. That is why the balance looks frozen. It nearly is.

The Real Numbers (Run For You)

The two paths, side by side. Minimum only: two hundred forty-eight months, twenty-two thousand, seven hundred sixty dollars paid in total, of which fourteen thousand, seven hundred sixty is interest. Flat three hundred a month: thirty-nine months, eleven thousand, six hundred seventy-one dollars total, of which three thousand, six hundred seventy-one is interest. The extra you commit is small — under a hundred dollars in month one, and less than that later, since the minimum keeps dropping. What it buys is eleven thousand and eighty-nine dollars back in your pocket and seventeen fewer years carrying this. And notice what happens the moment the payment is fixed instead of percentage-based: every dollar of progress makes the next month's interest smaller, so a fixed payment accelerates by itself. The minimum decelerates by itself. Those are opposite machines.

What To Do Today

Tonight, open your card's app and switch from minimum payment to a fixed autopay amount. Not a percentage — a number. Pick something you can hold every month even in a bad month, then never lower it as the balance falls; that's where all the speed comes from. If three hundred is too much, two hundred still cuts this from twenty years to about five. And if you have a card with a promotional zero percent balance transfer offer, run that math too — just set the fixed payment high enough to clear the balance before the promo window closes, because the rate after it closes is usually worse than the one you left.
