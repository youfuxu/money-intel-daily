Changing jobs and cashing out the old 401(k) feels like free money — it's the single most expensive button in personal finance. A $25,000 balance hands you about $15,750 after the 10% penalty and taxes.

## The Number That Changes Everything

A twenty-five thousand dollar 401k. He changed jobs, checked the box that said cash it out, and about fifteen thousand, seven hundred fifty dollars landed in his account. Left alone in the market for thirty-five years at eight percent, that same balance would have been three hundred sixty-nine thousand, six hundred thirty-four dollars. The cash-out cost him three hundred fifty-three thousand, eight hundred eighty-four dollars — and the penalty everyone warns you about is the smallest part of that number.

---

## Why This Matters

Meet Marcus. He's thirty, he just left a job he was ready to leave, and the exit paperwork asks a question nobody prepared him for: what would you like to do with your retirement account. Three options, one page, no explanation. Rolling it over means finding the new plan's account number and filling in a transfer form. Cashing it out means checking a box and getting money in ten days. He has a car repair, a credit card balance, and a moving deposit all landing in the same month. He checks the box. The twenty-five thousand felt like a bonus he forgot he had.

---

## How It Actually Works

Here's what actually happens when you check that box. First, the ten percent early withdrawal penalty, because he's under fifty-nine and a half — that's two thousand five hundred dollars, gone before anything else. Then the whole withdrawal counts as ordinary income for the year, so federal tax takes roughly twenty-two percent, five thousand five hundred dollars, and state income tax takes another five percent or so, twelve hundred fifty. Twenty-five thousand becomes about fifteen thousand seven hundred fifty. That's the visible cost, and it's the part people brace for. The invisible cost is the one that matters: every one of those dollars had thirty-five years of compounding ahead of it, and you can never buy those years back. Contribution limits are annual — you cannot go back and refill 2026 later.

---

## The Real Numbers (Run For You)

Run both paths. Path one, cash out: he keeps fifteen thousand, seven hundred fifty dollars today and the account is closed. Path two, roll it over into an IRA and never add another dollar: twenty-five thousand growing at eight percent a year for thirty-five years is three hundred sixty-nine thousand, six hundred thirty-four dollars. The gap between those two paths is three hundred fifty-three thousand, eight hundred eighty-four dollars. Put that against the reason for the withdrawal — a car repair, a card balance, a deposit — and the exchange rate is brutal: he traded roughly three hundred fifty thousand dollars of his sixties to solve a few thousand dollars of problems in his thirties. And notice this is the version where he never contributes again. Keep contributing and the gap gets wider, not narrower.

---

## What To Do Today

If you have an old 401k sitting at a job you already left, do one thing this week: call your current brokerage and ask them to start a direct rollover. Direct means the money moves institution to institution and never touches your bank account — that matters, because if it lands in your account first, your old employer is required to withhold twenty percent and you have sixty days to replace it out of pocket or it counts as a withdrawal anyway. The call takes about fifteen minutes and they handle the paperwork on their side. If you're staring at that exit form right now, the box you want is roll over, not cash out. That single checkbox is worth more than most raises you will ever negotiate.
